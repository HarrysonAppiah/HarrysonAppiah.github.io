There are some songs you listen to for entertainment.

Then there are songs that make you stop, listen again, and ask yourself: *Wait… are they talking about us?*

Damian “Jr. Gong” Marley and Nas's **“Patience”** is one of those songs for me.

The song is more than music. It feels like a critique of a society that has somehow become very good at solving problems that do not matter while ignoring the ones sitting right in front of us.

One line that has always stood out to me is:
<!--more-->

> “Some of the smartest dummies can't read the language of Egyptian mummies.”

It is a fascinating contradiction. We live in an age of extraordinary technological achievement. We can send machines into space, develop artificial intelligence, map the human genome, build skyscrapers that seem to touch the clouds, and communicate instantly with someone on the other side of the planet.

Yet somehow, we still struggle with some of the most basic human problems.

People are hungry.

People cannot afford housing.

People work full-time jobs and still struggle to pay their bills.

Healthcare can cost more than the illness itself.

And young people are entering adulthood carrying enormous amounts of debt while being told to simply “work harder.”

So I sometimes wonder: **What exactly are we progressing toward?**

We can plant a flag on the moon, but can we make sure a child has enough food to eat?

We can build increasingly sophisticated technologies, but can we build a society where an ordinary person working an honest job can afford a decent place to live?

We can spend billions researching what is beyond our planet, but why does providing basic healthcare remain such a difficult problem?

These aren't arguments against science, technology, exploration, or progress. Quite the opposite.

The ability to explore the universe is one of humanity's greatest achievements.

But progress should not only be measured by how far we can go.

It should also be measured by **how many people we take with us.**

## What Do We Choose to Care About?

One of the things I hear in “Patience” is a criticism of our priorities.

We often say that children are the future. Yet education systems struggle for resources, teachers are underpaid, and many young people grow up surrounded by problems they did not create.

We say that human life is priceless. Yet someone's ability to receive quality healthcare can depend heavily on how much money they have.

We celebrate billion-dollar companies while millions of people worry about making rent.

We celebrate record-breaking technological achievements while ordinary families are trying to figure out how to afford groceries.

And somehow, we have normalized this contradiction.

That is the part that bothers me.

Not that society has problems. Every society has problems.

It is that we sometimes seem to have the resources, intelligence, and technology to address many of them — but our priorities don't always reflect that.

## The Economics of Being “Patient”

Maybe this is why the title *Patience* resonates with me.

We are constantly being asked to be patient.

Be patient. Housing prices will eventually stabilize.

Be patient. Your salary will eventually catch up.

Be patient. The economy will get better.

Be patient. Healthcare will become more affordable.

Be patient. Your career will take off.

Be patient.

But patience becomes a strange concept when the cost of everything keeps increasing while the value of your paycheck seems to move in the opposite direction.

A house that once represented stability can now represent decades of debt.

Food — something as basic as feeding your family — has become another calculation at the grocery store.

Healthcare can turn an ordinary medical problem into a financial crisis.

And wages?

For many people, they simply haven't kept pace with the responsibilities attached to adulthood.

Rent.

Mortgage.

Food.

Utilities.

Insurance.

Transportation.

Childcare.

Healthcare.

Education.

Retirement.

Family responsibilities.

And then, somewhere in the middle of all of that, you're supposed to find time to “enjoy life.”

Sometimes it feels like adulthood is a subscription service that keeps adding features without asking whether you can afford the monthly payment.

## The Things We Don't See

Another part of the song that strikes me is its criticism of how the media portrays the world.

What gets shown to us?

What gets repeated?

What gets sensationalized?

What becomes entertainment?

There is power in deciding what people see — and equally powerful is deciding what they don't see.

If the only images people repeatedly see from a particular part of the world are poverty, violence, corruption, and suffering, eventually those images become the identity of that place in the minds of people who have never been there.

The complexity disappears.

The scientists disappear.

The entrepreneurs disappear.

The teachers disappear.

The parents raising their children disappear.

The ordinary people living ordinary lives disappear.

All that remains is the image.

And once an image becomes a stereotype, it becomes very difficult to replace.

## The Real Question

Perhaps the deeper question isn't whether humanity is progressing.

We clearly are.

The question is:

**Who is that progress actually serving?**

Because technological progress without social progress can create a strange kind of inequality.

We can have more advanced phones but less affordable housing.

More sophisticated cars but more expensive transportation.

More powerful computers but exhausted workers.

More information than any generation before us but less agreement about what is true.

More wealth than ever before but millions of people still struggling to meet basic needs.

That contradiction deserves more than a passing thought.

It deserves a conversation.

## Monday Musings

Maybe that is what I appreciate most about songs like *“Patience.”*

They don't necessarily give you the answers.

They make you uncomfortable enough to start asking better questions.

What do we value?

Who benefits from the systems we have built?

What problems are we willing to spend billions solving?

Which problems do we tell people to simply “be patient” about?

And perhaps most importantly:

**What happens when we become so focused on what is impressive that we forget what is important?**

I don't have all the answers.

But I do know this:

A society should not measure its greatness only by the height of its buildings, the size of its economy, or how far its technology can take us.

Maybe we should also measure it by something much simpler:

**Can ordinary people afford to live ordinary lives with dignity?**

That, to me, is a much more meaningful definition of progress.

And maybe that's what Monday Musings is about — taking a song, a conversation, or an ordinary observation and asking:

**What does this say about the world we are building?**
